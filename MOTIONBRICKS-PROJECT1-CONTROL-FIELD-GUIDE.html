Project 1 · field guide · actual CPU demo

Learn the model through its controls

The right panel is an experimental instrument. Some controls change the G1 planner’s inputs, some expose the targets it was given, and some only change playback or viewpoint. Use this guide with the recorded demo or a local installation to make predictions and check what the system actually does.

First, what is moving?

MotionBricks is not taking a text prompt and playing a finished movie. The current controller combines recent G1 pose context with a movement direction, a facing direction and one style asset. It places four nearby target poses, asks the learned model to predict a motion chunk, and uses the resulting root translations and 34 joint rotations as the animated reference. While movement continues, the server plans ahead from the current motion. The browser receives timestamped poses and interpolates them for display.

What to look at in the scene

Mint skeleton: generated animated referenceWarm ghost: target keyframeGold root/pelvis marker

The mint bones and joints are the MotionBricks output displayed through the Three.js viewer. The warm diamond-jointed ghosts are the actual placed pose constraints sent to the planner. Mint and orange floor traces show the generated and target root paths. The ground grid gives scale and direction; it is not a physics floor in this build. The camera follows the animated pelvis, so the skeleton remains visible even if a target is ahead or to one side.

Observation to make: after changing direction or style, do the target ghosts change first, and does the generated body motion then change? The ghosts are particularly useful for distinguishing an unexpected command/target from an unexpected model response.

Do not over-read the colors. The warm ghost is not a second robot or the “correct answer” pose for every generated frame. It is a small four-pose constraint window. The mint skeleton is not a skinned character and, with physics disabled, it is not an actuated physical G1.

Every control explained

01 Unavailable in this build

Live physics · GGML SONIC and Show collision geometry

Both checkboxes are visible but disabled in Project 1. This build set MOTIONBRICKS_ENABLE_PHYSICS=OFF and has no SONIC model or MuJoCo scene. MotionBricks currently supplies a kinematic reference: desired poses, with no actuator tracking, contact forces or physical fall simulation. If physics were separately installed, the demo could show a blue generated reference, green physical robot and optional collision shapes. None of that is being tested by these disabled controls now.

Ask: does the reference look plausible? Do not infer balance, ground forces or robot executability from this skeleton-only display.

02 Session

Reset session / Pause–Resume

Pause stops the server-owned timeline; Resume continues it. This is useful for freezing a pose while you inspect the target ghosts. Reset session starts a fresh simulation epoch, resets motion context and the timeline, and recenters the camera follow state. It is the fair starting point for comparing two control sequences. It does not reload or retrain the model.

Ask: if you reset, hold W for the same duration and select the same style, is the broad trajectory reproducible? Sampling can still produce variation; a visual test alone is not a strict numerical parity test.

03 Legend

Animated model / Target keyframes

The mint swatch marks the generated skeleton; the orange swatch marks target constraints. It is a legend, not a switch. The actual target pose is allowed to overlap the character or sit sideways after stopping or turning; the viewer does not move it to make the composition prettier. That is useful evidence about what the planner was asked to do.

04 Model input

Upstream pose style

The menu loads the 15 supplied .mbstyle assets. Each option displays a name and nominal speed in m/s. Selecting a different asset changes the controller’s style target and requests a new plan. It is not a text prompt, a newly trained model, or a guarantee that the character will exactly travel at the printed speed. When you release movement, the demo requests idle behavior regardless of the walking style you were exploring.

For a clean comparison, use the same movement direction and camera angle, run one style, reset, and repeat with another. Look at arm posture, torso lean, step timing, root path and the target ghost—not just whether the label changed.

05 Optional external asset

Authored Kimodo sequence / Play / Upload animation GLB

The selector is empty and Play is disabled until a compatible animation is available. Upload animation GLB accepts a Kimodo G1 g1skel34 skeleton-animation GLB at 30 FPS, no more than 16 MiB and 10 minutes. It does not retarget arbitrary characters or meshes. No such clip was supplied with Project 1.

After a validated upload, Play stitches a short entry blend to the authored clip, plays the clip without normal movement interruption, then hands the final poses back to MotionBricks for continuing locomotion. During authored playback the normal motion controls are locked. Uploads live in server memory until restart. This is a different experiment from style-conditioned live planning: a Kimodo clip is pre-authored playback, not MotionBricks inventing that sequence on the fly.

06 Inspector

Inspect placed target: T0–T3 slider

Moving this slider chooses which of the four already supplied target poses is fully visible. It does not move the target, alter the prediction, choose a future destination, or scrub the generated motion. The four target poses are consecutive at 30 FPS—about one tenth of a second from T0 to T3—so their root positions may be very close. The panel’s “Targets” line names the selected one.

Ask: do the ghosts sit ahead when walking forward? What happens to their placement after a direction change or stop? If a turn looks odd, inspect the targets before blaming the network or graphics.

07 Inspector

Overlay all four consecutive poses

This displays T0, T1, T2 and T3 together at different opacities instead of showing only the slider-selected pose. It makes a short pose trajectory visible. T0–T3 are not four distant waypoints and not the full predicted 30-frame motion. The overlay changes only the viewer; the model receives the same constraint window either way.

Ask: is the local target sequence internally coherent—moving in one direction and changing pose smoothly—or are you seeing a sudden orientation/position change?

08 Viewpoint, with input consequence

Reset camera / drag to orbit / wheel to zoom

Dragging the scene changes camera yaw and pitch; the wheel changes distance. Reset camera restores the default view and recenters tracking. These do not edit the model weights. However, W/A/S/D are camera-relative: after orbiting, W maps to a new world-space travel direction. If you are currently holding or latching movement, a camera change can therefore cause a new movement command and plan. The camera follows the pelvis, not the target ghost.

Ask: after a 90° orbit, does W move the character away from the camera in the new view? That tests the input coordinate transform, not the neural model alone.

09 Controller input

W/A/S/D pad, physical keys and facing arrows

Hold physical W/A/S/D to travel relative to the current camera. The on-screen W/A/S/D buttons latch: tap a direction to keep moving, tap the active direction again to stop. Space or Escape clears movement. Diagonal input is normalized so two simultaneous keys do not automatically double speed. The left/right arrow keys rotate facing without changing the current travel vector. Ordinary movement tends to set facing to travel direction, so use the arrows deliberately when studying independent facing.

Releasing all movement asks the demo for idle with zero target speed, then it holds the completed stop pose. Continued travel asks for new plans ahead of the current motion. A stop may still take time to become visible because the current reference, planning, stream and viewer buffer have boundaries.

Ask: can you separate three events: when the pad lights up, when the target ghosts change, and when the mint skeleton visibly changes course? Those are different stages of the system.

10 Special request

Jump / J

Jump requests one bounded MotionBricks transition with a temporarily higher target speed (5.0 m/s). The direction comes from current movement, or facing if stationary. It uses the existing style’s target poses; it does not add a guaranteed vertical arc or a separate jump model. In the repository’s fixed walking regression the result was a short hop, but that foot clearance is not guaranteed for every style or starting pose. The transition is allowed to play before normal walking resumes.

Ask: do both feet clear the floor, does the pose remain coherent, and does ordinary movement resume without a snap? This is a motion-quality test, not proof of physically feasible jumping.

11 Telemetry

Status / Plan / Targets / Backend

Status reports whether the viewer is streaming, buffering, paused, waiting for a plan, disconnected or spectating another tab. The line above it says “50 Hz server stream” as the nominal design rate and reports viewer playback underruns. It is not a live measured FPS display. Plan in streaming mode shows the active style and server time; that number is the simulation clock, not planner latency in milliseconds. Targets identifies the selected ghost or says all four are overlaid. Backend: native GGML means this is using the native inference library, but the label alone does not distinguish CPU from Vulkan. This installation was launched explicitly with -device cpu.

If Status says “Spectating” and motion buttons are disabled, another browser tab controls the one shared session. Return to the controlling tab. If it is gone, stop and restart the demo, leaving one tab open.

Six experiments that teach more than the graphics

Change one cause at a time. Write down your prediction before you touch the control. The live demo is observational; these tests help form hypotheses but are not formal model benchmarks.

Experiment A · control loop

Walk, replan, stop

  1. Reset session, choose walk, and leave the camera at its default.
  2. Hold W for about five seconds, then release it. Watch the warm targets, mint root path and Status line.
  3. Repeat with the on-screen W button, tapping once to start and again to stop.

Look for: the target window moving ahead, repeated continuation of the walk, and a stable stop. Meaning: if the pad changes but the ghosts do not, investigate command delivery; if ghosts change but the skeleton does not, investigate planning or playback.

Experiment B · style conditioning

Same command, two styles

  1. With the default camera, run walk while holding W for a few seconds.
  2. Reset session; choose walk_zombie or stealth_walk; use the same W input.
  3. Compare target ghosts, arm posture, torso lean, foot rhythm and travel, not just the name in the menu.

Look for: whether a style changes pose as well as pace. Limit: sampling and initial pose context may vary; the printed speed differs for some styles.

Experiment C · constraints

Inspect the four-frame target window

  1. Pause while walking and turn on Overlay all four consecutive poses.
  2. Turn the overlay off and move the T0–T3 slider through each pose.
  3. Resume, turn, then pause again and inspect the new target window.

Look for: a short local target sequence rather than four far-away destinations. Meaning: the slider is an observation instrument; changing it should not change the generated motion.

Experiment D · coordinate frames

Orbit before walking

  1. Reset camera and press W; note the world-grid direction.
  2. Stop. Drag to orbit roughly a quarter turn, then press W again.
  3. Use Reset camera and repeat.

Look for: W moving away from the camera in each view, even when world direction changes. Meaning: distinguish the camera-to-world input transform from the model’s ability to produce a turn.

Experiment E · travel versus facing

Turn the body without a new travel vector

  1. With a clear view, use the left or right arrow while the character is stationary.
  2. Observe the facing and target ghosts, then start a short walk.

Look for: whether facing responds separately from translation. Limit: pressing a movement key subsequently tends to align facing with travel; that is controller policy.

Experiment F · latency and continuity

Test the real weak point

  1. With one Chrome tab and few other heavy apps running, hold W, then turn, stop and start again.
  2. Note whether Status says Waiting for motion planner or Buffering streamed poses, and whether the playback underrun count rises.
  3. Compare visible input-to-motion delay with the numerical planner times in .state/demo-8080.log.

Look for: where the delay lives. A 300 ms planner call, a 600 ms visible delay and an underrun counter describe different phenomena. The first measured walk plan here took 461 ms; one eight-second probe advanced only about 20 server ticks per wall-clock second, so sustained speed remains an open performance question.
